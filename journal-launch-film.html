How to plan a launch film people finish

Most launch videos lose their audience in the first few seconds. The fix is in the plan, not the edit.

Launch films usually fail for the same reason: they are planned like an ad and watched like a feed. The first seconds go on a logo, a mood and a slow push-in, while the viewer is deciding whether to keep scrolling. By the time the product appears, most of them are gone.

A film people finish is mostly decided before anyone opens an editing timeline. This is how we plan them.

Write the one sentence first

Before the script, write the single sentence a viewer should be able to repeat afterwards. “It finds the subscriptions you forgot about.” “It answers your shop’s WhatsApp while you work.” Every shot either supports that sentence or gets cut.

Open on the problem, not the brand

The first three seconds should show a situation the viewer recognizes, ideally without sound, because many will watch muted. The brand can arrive at the end, once it means something.

Show the product doing one thing

Resist the feature tour. Pick the moment where the product changes someone’s day and show it clearly, at a readable pace, with real screens. One clear moment beats eight fast cuts nobody can follow.

Plan the length around where it will live

A homepage hero, a social post, a sales deck and a product-launch post all want different lengths and aspect ratios. Decide where the film will run before you storyboard, then plan the cutdowns from the start rather than chopping the master afterwards.

Design for sound off, and sound on

Captions and on-screen type should carry the story by themselves. Music and voice should add to it, not hold it up. If the film makes sense on mute, it works everywhere.

End with one action

Close on the sentence you wrote at the start and one next step: try it, book a call, read the launch post. Two calls to action halve the response to both.

Ship it with the page

A film is strongest when it lands alongside the page it points to. That is why we plan our Launch Bundle as one project: the site and the film share a script, a visual language and a launch day. The video makes the promise, and the page lets people act on it straight away.

— Ajit Patel and Daksh Sahu, Divine Studio
