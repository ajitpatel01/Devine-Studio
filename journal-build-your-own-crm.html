When should a team build its own CRM?

Most teams shouldn’t. A few should, and they usually find out late. Here is how to tell which one you are.

Every few months a founder asks us to build them a CRM. Usually the honest answer is that they shouldn’t, at least not yet. Off-the-shelf CRMs are cheap, well tested and good at the common case: track a contact, log a call, move a deal through stages. If your sales process looks like that, pay the subscription and spend your engineering time on your product.

Some teams really are better off with their own system, though. They tend to find out late, after a year of exports, spreadsheet sidecars and Zapier chains that only one person understands. Here are the signals we look for.

1. Your process isn’t a pipeline

CRMs assume a deal moves left to right. Plenty of businesses don’t work that way. A recruiting firm has two sides to every placement. A logistics company tracks shipments, not deals. A clinic manages patients through repeat visits. If you have bent stages, custom objects and automations until the tool barely resembles itself, you are already paying the cost of a custom system without getting the benefits.

2. The real work happens outside the CRM

Look at where your team spends the day. If the CRM is the place they update after the work is done, rather than the place where the work happens, it has become a reporting tax. A system shaped around the actual job (quoting, scheduling, onboarding, approvals) gets used because it saves time, and the data stays accurate as a side effect.

3. You are paying per seat for people who barely log in

Per-seat pricing makes sense for salespeople who live in the tool. It makes less sense for the operations, finance and field staff who need two screens and one button. When most of your licenses go to occasional users, a lean internal tool with the right permissions is often cheaper to run, as well as simpler.

4. Your data needs to join other data

The most valuable questions usually cross systems: which customers that came from a given channel renewed, which accounts raise the most support tickets, which deals stalled after a pricing change. If answering them means exporting from three tools and reconciling by hand, you have outgrown the boundaries of an off-the-shelf CRM.

What we would do first

Before anyone writes code, we map the process as it actually runs: who touches a record, what they need to see, and what decision they make. That usually takes a short, paid discovery. Sometimes the answer is to configure the CRM you already have properly. Sometimes it is a small custom layer on top of it. Now and then it is a purpose-built system, a CRM or an HRMS, that replaces several tools.

If you do build, build less than you think. Start with the one workflow that hurts most, ship it to the people who do that job, and grow from there. A custom system that does three things well beats one that copies every feature of the tool you left.

The test is simple. If your team would notice and complain on the day the new system went away, it was worth building.

— Ajit Patel and Daksh Sahu, Divine Studio
